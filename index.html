Crate Rescue: Detroit

Builders in Detroit buy more materials than they need. Help get the extra crates to builders who need them, before they’re thrown away.

  1. The problem: hidden crates

    The extra crates sit in builders’ yards, and nobody keeps track of them. That’s called blind inventory. After 4 weeks they get thrown away.

  2. The eyes: drones

    A drone flies over each yard and scans it, so the hidden crates show up for everyone.

  3. The brain: an AI dispatcher

    The AI sees what the drone found and suggests where each crate should go. Robot trucks deliver them. The city checks every trip, and the AI learns from mistakes.

    What happened

      For grown-ups: what this game is about

      The problem

      Construction sites routinely order more material than they install. The surplus sits in their yards untracked, and much of it is eventually thrown away. This untracked surplus is blind inventory. In the game, crates start hidden and go to the landfill after four weeks.

      The proposed fix

      Autonomous drones measure the yards, and a language-model dispatcher reads that data and proposes where each crate should go. Autonomous trucks then carry the crates to the builder who needs them, or to a shared reuse warehouse that passes a few crates a week on to neighborhood builders. In this game the dispatcher is built in: a rule of thumb that stands in for the model’s proposals. It matches visible crates to the nearest builder who needs them, and saves old crates in the warehouse.

      Why the city can say no

      The dispatcher judges distance in a straight line, as it would from a map, but trucks have to follow real streets, including a real road closure. So a proposal can fail. The failures are shown where they happen: too far (the truck runs out of delivery time), too heavy (more than six crates), not needed (the builder didn’t order that), full (the warehouse has no room), and can’t see them (unscanned crates can’t be dispatched). When a trip is refused for distance, the dispatcher records the lesson and stops proposing it.

      The score

      Saved counts crates delivered to builders plus crates stored at the warehouse. Thrown out counts crates that aged out. Refused counts moves the city rejected. The end screen compares your season with doing nothing, where every extra crate is lost.

      What’s real

      • The six sites are real Detroit projects under way in 2026: the Henry Ford Hospital tower, The Claire, Midtown West, the U-M Center for Innovation, the JW Marriott at Water Square and AlumniFi Field.
      • The Reuse Warehouse stands for the Architectural Salvage Warehouse of Detroit, a nonprofit that deconstructs buildings and resells what it saves.
      • The street map is a simplified trace of central Detroit. The closure near AlumniFi Field is MDOT’s 2026 Michigan Ave rebuild in Corktown. Other road work is made up and changes each week.
      • Crate counts, the six-crate truck, the seven-kilometer delivery limit and the four-week rule are simplified for play.

      Crate Rescue: Detroit is a design study of blind inventory in construction, made for ARC327R. The sites and the Michigan Ave rebuild are real; crate counts and rules are simplified for play.
